# ADR-0047: Time: a document's life in formulas, what is due, and timed transitions

- Status: Accepted; amends ADR-0008 (a transition may fire by time), ADR-0013 (four more system variables), ADR-0014 (four more system columns; engine schema 5), ADR-0018 (`after` and `at` on a transition), ADR-0019 (the functions of a document's life), ADR-0026 (the event of an entry says how the move came about), ADR-0040 (what entering a state is, and the `_states` view), ADR-0044 (the wake-up row)
- Date: 2026-11-11

## Context

The owner ruled on 2026-11-10 that Phase 4 builds time in two halves,
the first before the second (ADR-0044, "Time"):

1. *Conditions on time*: whether a document could, or should, move from
   one state to another.
2. *Timers and a scheduler*: firing a transition at a given time, with
   its retries.

What runs today. A guard is a formula and may read `TODAY()`, `NOW()`
and any date the document holds, so `TODAY() > due` can be written.
`Transitions` says whether a document could move, at the database's
clock or at a moment given with `At` (ADR-0042). An eventless transition
with such a guard says that it should, and it moves when a commit
touches the document, not when the time comes.

What is missing.

- A formula reads nothing of the document's own life. It has `STATE()`
  and `ID()`. It cannot say "seven days in this state", "the person who
  created it may not approve it" or "back to whoever changed it last".
  The facts are stored: every version keeps who made it, when and in
  which state, and the `_states` view lists every stay in a state. No
  function reaches them.
- Nothing lists the documents that would move now. A caller asks
  document by document.
- Nothing fires by itself.

The owner gave two things on 2026-11-11. They are items 1 and 2. After
reading the record he added a third, which is in items 3 and 6: every
entry into a state is recorded, and it can be asked when a document
first entered a state and by whom, and when it last left it. Asked
whether that is enough, the assistant proposed two more, which he took
on the same day: where a document came from, and how each move came
about. They are in items 3 to 5.

## Decision

1. **A timer is written on a transition** (the owner's ruling). A timer
   always moves the document. A reminder is a move too: a transition
   from a state back into itself, with the reminder as the action on it.
   A rule that fires by time and leaves the document where it is is not
   built.

2. **A formula reads the life of its document** (the owner's
   requirement): when and by whom it was created and last changed, when
   and by whom it entered the state it is in, and for any state when and
   by whom it first and last entered it and left it, how often and how
   long it was there, and who first and last changed it there. Items 3 to 7
   are that family. Process mining reads the same facts across all
   documents from the views, so a function and a view give one answer.

### The first half: checking

3. **What entering a state is.** A document enters a state when it is
   created in it and when a transition lands in it, named or eventless,
   from another state or from the same one. Each entry begins a stay,
   and ends the stay before it: a transition into the same state leaves
   it and enters it at one moment.

   **Every entry is recorded** (the owner's requirement), in three
   places that say the same. The version the entry makes is a row of the
   history, with the state, the moment and who made it. The log has its
   event, `created` or `transition`, with the state left, the state
   entered and the name of the transition. And the `_states` view has
   one row for the stay it begins.

   **The event says how the move came about**, under `origin`:
   `command` when somebody asked for it, by creating the document or
   taking a named transition; `action` when a rule of a document asked
   for it through the outbox; `guard` when an eventless transition
   fired in the chain of a commit; `timer` when a timed transition did
   (item 10). So the log tells the steps somebody asked for from the
   ones the process took by itself.

   **The view of stays shows all of it.** ADR-0040, item 7, began a
   visit only where the state differs from the previous version's, so a
   return into the same state was folded into the stay before it; the
   view now has a row for every entry. It
   gains, for the entry that began the stay, `entered_by`, who made it,
   `entered_from`, the state the document came from, `entered_via`, the
   name of the transition, and `entered_how`, its origin; and for the
   move that ended it `left_by`, `left_via` and `left_how`. Nothing of
   this is ever changed or removed: the history and the log are
   append-only (ADR-0006).

4. **The document keeps who, and where from.** Four system columns join
   the seven: `created_by`, `updated_by` and `state_entered_by`, the
   principal of the commit that created the document, made its current
   version, and began its current stay; and `state_entered_from`, the
   state it was in before, blank for a document still in the state it
   was created in. The three of who are blank when that commit's command
   named no principal. What the outbox does it does as the person whose
   command queued it (ADR-0044), so that person is who. They are
   columns of the document table, of its history, of the views and of
   the class as a `LOOKUP` reads it (ADR-0046), and fields of the wire
   document. The names are reserved. The engine schema is 5.

5. **The functions of the row.** They take no argument and cost nothing:
   the commit has the row in hand.

   | Function | Answers | Type |
   |---|---|---|
   | `CREATEDAT()`, `CREATEDBY()` | when the document was created, and by whom | datetime, text |
   | `UPDATEDAT()`, `UPDATEDBY()` | when its current version was made, and by whom | datetime, text |
   | `ENTEREDAT()`, `ENTEREDBY()`, `ENTEREDFROM()` | when it entered the state it is in, who moved it there, and which state it came from | datetime, text, text |
   | `VERSION()` | its version | number |

   "Seven days in this state" is `NOW() >= ADDDAYS(ENTEREDAT(), 7)`.
   "It came back from review" is `ENTEREDFROM() = "review"`; after a
   transition into the same state it is that state. The owner's
   `CurrentEditor` is `USER()`, which exists.

6. **The functions of a state.** They take the name of a state. A name
   written as text is checked against the class's states when the class
   is defined.

   | Function | Answers | Type |
   |---|---|---|
   | `FIRSTENTERED(state)`, `FIRSTENTEREDBY(state)` | when the document first entered the state, and who moved it there; blank if it never did | datetime, text |
   | `LASTENTERED(state)`, `LASTENTEREDBY(state)` | the same of its last entry | datetime, text |
   | `FIRSTLEFT(state)`, `FIRSTLEFTBY(state)` | when it first left the state, and who moved it out; blank if it never left | datetime, text |
   | `LASTLEFT(state)`, `LASTLEFTBY(state)` | the same of the last time it left; for a document that is in the state, the time before | datetime, text |
   | `ENTRIES(state)` | how many times it entered it | number |
   | `DAYSIN(state)` | the days it has spent there in all, with fractions, up to now when it is there | number |
   | `FIRSTEDITOR(state)`, `LASTEDITOR(state)` | who made the first and the last version of the document while it was in the state, versions made with no principal left out | text |

   They read the document's own events and versions, once per command
   however many of them a command evaluates, through the index the
   event log already has. They see the document's life up to the commit
   being computed, the entry that commit makes included.

7. **Where they may be written.** They are context functions, like
   `STATE()` and `ID()`: in a default, a triggered variable, a guard, a
   rule, an access rule and a right, and not in an auto formula, whose
   value must follow from the document's own values (ADR-0015). They
   speak of the document the formula belongs to. Another document is
   read with `LOOKUP`, where `created_by`, `updated_by`,
   `state_entered_by`, `state_entered_from` and the three moments are
   columns.

8. **What is due.** `Due(class)` lists the documents of a class that
   would move if a commit touched them now, or at the moment given with
   `At`: each with its state, the state it would land in, and the moment
   since which that has been so when the engine knows it. It evaluates
   the guards of the eventless and the timed transitions as one filter
   per state where the guards translate to SQL (ADR-0038), and document
   by document where they do not. `Transitions` shows a timed transition
   with the moment it fires.

### The second half: firing

9. **`after` and `at`.** A transition may carry one of them:

   ```json
   {"from": "sent", "to": "overdue", "after": "30d"}
   {"from": "sent", "to": "overdue", "at": "ADDDAYS(due, 1)"}
   {"name": "remind", "from": "waiting", "to": "waiting", "after": "3d"}
   ```

   `after` is a length of time in the state: a whole number and `d`,
   `h`, `m` or `s`. A day is 24 hours. `at` is a formula over the
   document's own values and the functions of items 5 and 6, giving a
   date, which means the first instant of that day in UTC, or a
   datetime; it may not read the clock or the user. A blank `at` never
   comes. A guard beside either is still asked.

10. **A timed transition is a condition on time that the engine
    promises to look at.** Its meaning is that of an eventless
    transition whose guard also says "the time has come": it fires in
    the chain of any commit made once the time has come (ADR-0040).
    What the engine adds is the commit: when a document enters the
    state, the same transaction queues a wake-up row for each timed
    transition that leaves it, due at its moment (ADR-0044, item 1).
    So a timer that the worker missed fires at the next commit, and a
    stopped worker loses nothing.

11. **The wake-up.** When it is due the worker looks at the document.
    If it is gone, in another state or in a later stay, or the moment
    has moved to later, the row is done and writes no event. Otherwise
    it commits, and the timed transition fires in that commit's chain.
    A failure is retried like any row, and a row that is dead is listed
    like any dead action. When a commit changes a value that an `at`
    reads, it queues a row for the new moment; the old one finds nothing
    to do.

12. **Who acts.** The wake-up carries the principal of the commit that
    began the stay, as ADR-0044, item 11, has it for every row. The move
    is made as that person: `USER()` in its rules is theirs, and what
    those rules do to other documents is done with their rights. Its
    event names them as actor, the event that began the stay as cause,
    and the row's key. The move itself asks no right: it is the
    document's own transition, and a guard is the only thing that stops
    one. (Which commit that is when a date was changed since, the owner
    ruled on 2026-11-12; it is under "Details settled while building".)

13. **A name on a timed transition** lets a rule hang on it, as on
    `remind` above. It also makes it a named transition like any other:
    a person may take it before its time, under its guard and its
    access rule.

14. **What is refused when the class is defined.** Both `after` and
    `at` on one transition. A timed transition out of a terminal state.
    An `at` on a transition into its own state, which would fire again
    at once: `after` is how a stay repeats. An `after` that is not a
    length of time. An `at` that reads the clock, the user or another
    document.

15. **The clock is the database's.** A timer fires by the clock a
    commit runs by (ADR-0042). A simulation is a copy of the file
    opened with `Options.Now` and a `Drain`.

16. **What waits.** A rule that fires by time without moving the
    document. `schedule` on a rule and on a variable, which recompute
    many documents at once and belong to the analytical engine
    (ADR-0044). Working days and time zones: a day is 24 hours of UTC.
    A length of time as a type of the formula language. Who last
    changed one variable: the history has it, and no function reads it.

    And one thing for Phase 5, noted here on the owner's word of
    2026-11-11: whether an actor is an agent or a person, and on whose
    behalf it acts. The engine records the id the host program gives
    and nothing more, so the log cannot yet say which steps of a
    process an agent took. It belongs with the principal and the
    access rules.

17. **The conformance suite.** `conformance/formula/context.json` gains
    the functions of items 5 and 6, with the life of a document given
    inline. `conformance/statechart/` gains the entry into the same
    state and the `_states` view. `conformance/rules/` gains `timers`,
    scripts that move the clock: a transition after a time, at a date
    the document holds, a reminder that repeats, a date that is changed
    while the document waits, a document that leaves before its time,
    and a worker that was stopped.

## Details settled while building

The first part of the checking half built items 3, 4, 5 and 7 on
2026-11-11: what entering a state is and the `_states` view, the four
system columns, the origin of an entry, and the functions of the row.
The functions of a state, item 6, and `Due`, item 8, follow, and then
the firing half. These points were settled.

- **The origin is kept as a small number.** The events table holds 1 to
  4 for `command`, `action`, `guard` and `timer`; every view and every
  read gives the word. A text the write step hands to SQLite costs a
  copy, a number does not.
- **The four columns stand after the variables** in the document table
  and in its history. What SQLite pays to read a column grows with its
  place in the row, and these are read least. A statement names its
  columns, so nothing else follows from the order.
- **Leaving a state.** For a rule and a triggered variable of an exit
  or of a transition, `STATE()` is the state left (ADR-0043). The entry
  goes with it: `ENTEREDAT()`, `ENTEREDBY()` and `ENTEREDFROM()` tell of
  the stay that ends. On the entry they tell of the stay that begins.
- **A commit by nobody** leaves `updated_by` blank, and the others when
  it created the document or moved it. `VERSION()` and the moments are
  blank where there is no document.
- **A program knows whether it reads the life.** Only a formula that
  calls one of the functions is given the row's life, and the listing
  of open transitions reads who made the document only for a class
  whose formulas ask. The stay a transition ended is put before the
  rules and the triggered variables of the state left only in a class
  with such a formula. A class that uses none of this does what it did.
- **What it costs, and how the write step paid for it.** Measured on
  the machine of ADR-0035: every stored column costs about one
  microsecond of a commit in this build, so four more columns in two
  tables cost a transition about 4 µs of 56. The write step was changed
  to earn that back. An existing document is updated where it was
  written with an insert that turned into an update; a commit that left
  every value as it was writes the system columns alone; each value is
  boxed once for the current row, the history row and the event, where
  it was boxed for each; the history row is written without its empty
  `valid_to`; and the writer reads a row into lists it keeps. A
  transition now allocates 159 times where it allocated 168, and the
  outbox benchmark 463 where 490. The bytes of a commit rose, by 3% for
  a transition and just under 5% for the outbox benchmark: the four
  values themselves.
- **What was tried and is slower.** Letting SQLite write the history
  row as a copy of the current one, and close the version before it and
  write the event's `event_docs` row from triggers, saves the write
  step three statements and a third of its bytes, and costs time: 63 µs
  against 58 for a transition. In the pure-Go SQLite the work of a
  trigger outweighs the call it saves. It is not built.
- **The engine schema is 5**, for the four columns and the origin.

The second part of the checking half built item 6, the functions of a
state, on 2026-11-11. `Due`, item 8, follows, and then the firing half.
These points were settled.

- **Who is an editor.** Who created the document in the state, and who
  changed it there with a set or a reset. Who moved it into the state
  or out of it is not one, with values in the same command or without:
  `LASTENTEREDBY` and `LASTLEFTBY` say that. So a purchase that review
  sends back goes to `LASTEDITOR("draft")`, who last wrote the draft,
  and not to who sent it back or sent it on. A refresh computes again
  and a migration moves the document to a new version of the class;
  neither is a change of the document, so an agent that refreshes
  documents does not become their editor. A change made by nobody is
  left out.
- **The event of a creation names its state**, under `to_state`, as the
  event of a transition does. The log alone then tells every entry,
  which the functions read it for.
- **What a function sees while a command runs.** The access rule of a
  transition and its guard see the life as the log has it. Everything
  after the move sees the move too: a triggered variable, a rule and an
  eventless transition that follows. So `ENTRIES("review")` on the
  entry of `review` counts that entry, and `LASTLEFT` of the state just
  left is now. A set tells of itself the same way: its maker is the
  last editor in its own rules. This holds for every formula a commit
  runs, one of a rule that creates a document for each element of a
  list too.
- **A state that is not one.** A name written as text that the class
  does not have is refused when the class is defined, in any case of
  letters but the class's own. A name that is computed and names no
  state answers as a state the document was never in: blank, and 0 for
  `ENTRIES` and `DAYSIN`. Where there is no document every one is blank.
- **Where they are refused.** In a read rule, a read right and the
  `where` of a filter. Those run for every row of a query, and these
  functions read the events of one document. The `_states` view has the
  same facts for all documents, and a triggered variable keeps an
  answer where a rule needs it. In an auto formula they are refused as
  every context function is.
- **Over no document row** the SQL dialects are given the states as one
  JSON text and read the named state's facts from it, so the three
  evaluators answer the same. Over the rows of a query they are not
  translated.
- **What it costs**, measured on the machine of ADR-0035 for a document
  with a hundred versions, of which 26 are moves. A class that asks for
  no editor reads the creation and the moves alone: 42 µs. One that
  asks for an editor reads every change: 85 µs, and its benchmark is in
  the gate. Both are one read for a command, and none for a create. The
  read grows with the document's history, a little under a microsecond
  for each version it hands over; the
  campaign of step 8 measures long histories. A class that calls none
  of these functions reads nothing and keeps nothing.
- **The write step boxes the name of a state once.** The event of a
  creation now carries a state, which cost the write step an
  allocation. Every commit writes its document's state, and an entry
  the state left and the state entered beside it; the names are now
  boxed when first written and kept. A transition allocates 155 times
  where it allocated 159, and the outbox benchmark 459 where 463.
- **The suite.** The cases of the functions stand in
  `conformance/formula/context.json` with the events of a document
  given inline, and every evaluator folds them with the same code.

The third part of the checking half built item 8, `Due`, on
2026-11-12, which closes that half. The firing half follows. These
points were settled.

- **What "would move" means.** A document is due when the guard of an
  eventless transition leaving its state is TRUE over the document as
  it is stored, at the moment asked. It is what the preview of a commit
  that changes nothing answers for one document, and the tests hold the
  two against each other. Every commit follows its chain to the end, so
  a document at rest becomes due only through what lies outside it: the
  clock, a document or a table that a guard looks up, who asks, or a
  new version of the class.
- **The state it would move to is the first move**: the target of the
  first eventless transition, in the order of the class, whose guard
  holds. What follows that move, its rules and any further eventless
  transition, is the commit's to work out, since a rule may change what
  the next guard reads. `Preview` answers it for one document.
- **What moves a due document.** Any commit that touches it. `Refresh`
  with no variables is a commit that changes nothing and follows the
  chain, so it is the touch to use. `Due` itself writes nothing.
- **Who asks.** `USER()` and `INGROUP` in a guard are those of the
  caller of `Due`, as they are those of the committer in a commit. A
  guard reads every variable whatever the caller may read: it is the
  engine's condition, as in a commit, and the list tells the caller
  only that the document is due, as `Transitions` tells whether a guard
  holds.
- **A document in a terminal state is never due.** It is frozen
  (ADR-0039) and a commit that touched it would be refused, though a
  transition to another terminal state may leave its state.
- **One filter for each transition, and a whole state at a time.** Item
  8 says one filter per state. A state that two eventless transitions
  leave gets two statements, one for each guard, and the first in the
  order of the class names the target where both hold. Each statement
  can then use the index of what its own guard compares. When one guard
  of a state does not translate to SQL (it looks another document or a
  table up, or calls a function of a state), every document of that
  state is evaluated one by one, since the order of the guards decides
  where a document moves.
- **A document that an older version of the class computed** is
  evaluated as the commit that adopted it would (ADR-0039): its values
  moved to the current version by name and every formula computed
  again, then the guards. The filter reads the documents of the current
  version alone, since it reads what is stored. If the recalculation
  fails the commit would be refused, and the document is not due. A
  class at its first version has no such documents and does not look.
  A class that was defined again looks, state by state, until a state
  holds none, and then never again for that state: a document enters a
  state by a commit, and a commit writes the current version. `Migrate`
  brings every live document up at once.
- **The bound.** One call lists at most 10,000 documents, the bound of
  a filter (ADR-0038), in the order of the class's states and, within a
  state, of the ids. A caller that touches what it gets asks again
  until the list is empty.
- **The moment since which a document has been due** is not in the list
  yet. The engine knows it for a timed transition alone, where it is the
  moment the timer names; a guard does not say when it began to hold.
  It comes with the firing half, as does the timed transition in
  `Transitions`.
- **A comparison with the clock now uses an index.** The filter
  compiler gave SQLite a comparison on the bare column, which an index
  can serve, only where a variable was compared with a literal
  (ADR-0038). A guard on time compares with the clock: `TODAY() > due`.
  The compiler now does the same for a date, a datetime or a number
  variable compared with any operand that reads no document, such as
  `TODAY()`, `ADDDAYS(NOW(), -30)` or `2 + 1`. The exact predicate still
  stands beside it, so the answer is the same, and a blank on either
  side compares as zero as before. This holds for every filter, `Query`
  and `Count` too. The statement of `Due` asks for its order in a way
  that lets SQLite use that index and not walk the whole class in the
  order of its ids.
- **What it costs**, measured on the machine of ADR-0035, on disk,
  over 100,000 documents in one state of which 100 are due
  (`BenchmarkDue`). With the guard `TODAY() > due` and an index on
  `due`: 90 µs. Before the two changes above it was 12 ms, the whole
  class read. The same guard with no index on `due`: 8.2 ms. A guard
  that does not translate, here one on `DAYSIN`, costs 9.3 µs for each
  document of its state, read and evaluated one by one under the
  writer's lock for the time one document takes; a document with a long
  history costs its read of the log on top (item 6). When every one of
  the 100,000 documents is due, the call lists its 10,000 in 75 ms:
  SQLite then sorts the ids of all that match. The budget of the record
  is the indexed filter's, 10 ms over 1,000,000 documents, and step 8
  measures it at that size.
- **The suite** is `conformance/statechart/due.json`, with a `due` step
  in the scripts.

The firing half began on 2026-11-12 with the timed transition itself:
`after` and `at` on a transition and what is refused (items 9, 13 and
14), its firing in the chain of a commit (item 10, its meaning), the
moment in `Transitions` and "since when" in `Due` (item 8). After this
part a timed transition fires in the first commit that touches the
document once its time has come. The wake-up, with which the engine
makes that commit itself (items 10 to 12), and the suite of item 17
follow and close the step. These points were settled.

- **What an `after` is.** A whole number above zero and one of `d`,
  `h`, `m` and `s`, written together: `30d`, `12h`, `90m`, `45s`. No
  fraction, no sum of units, and no week, month or year: a month has no
  fixed length. At most a thousand years. Zero is refused: such a
  transition would fire in the commit that enters the state, and an
  eventless transition says that.
- **What an `at` may read.** The document's own values and the
  functions of items 5 and 6. Item 14 refuses the clock, the user and
  another document; a table is refused too, since it changes with no
  commit of the document, and the engine works the moment out again
  only when the document is committed. A date looked up is kept in a
  triggered variable, and the `at` names that. An `at` that can only be
  blank is refused.
- **A timed transition needs neither a name nor a guard.** Its time is
  its condition.
- **Its place in the chain.** The eventless and the timed transitions
  leaving a state are asked in the order of the class, and the first
  that fires is taken; the chain goes on from the state it enters. One
  commit may so fire a reminder and then an escalation, each a version
  with its event, under the bound of eight (ADR-0008).
- **When the time has come.** At the second of the moment and from then
  on. A date is 00:00:00 UTC of its day. A moment in the past has come:
  a document created with a date that has passed moves in the commit
  that creates it, and a commit that sets the date into the past moves
  the document.
- **A moment that cannot be worked out never comes**: an `at` that is
  blank, or whose formula fails (ADR-0019).
- **The event of a timed transition** carries the transition's name when
  it has one and the origin `timer`, in whoever's commit it fires. Like
  every transition of a chain it names the command's event as its
  cause. Taken by a command before its time, a named one has the origin
  `command`.
- **Its rules.** A timed transition with a name raises the trigger of
  that name when it fires in the chain, between the exit of the state
  and the entry, as when a command takes it; a triggered variable on
  `transition:<name>` is computed again.
- **No right is asked** when it fires in a chain, as item 12 says of
  the wake-up: the access rule of a named timed transition is asked of
  a command that takes it by name, and of nobody else. An access rule
  of `FALSE` therefore makes a transition that only its time takes.
- **A stay that the command itself begins counts from the command's
  moment.** In a commit that is the time of the entry. A preview at a
  chosen moment stamps what it computes with the database's clock
  (ADR-0042) and asks what a commit at its moment would do; for that
  commit the stay would begin at that moment, so an `after` out of it
  has not come, and a reminder fires once in a preview however far
  ahead it looks.
- **`Transitions`** gives a timed transition `fires_at`, the moment in
  seconds, past or to come. One without a name is listed for that
  moment alone, since nobody takes it: it is not enabled and has no
  code, and it is left out when its moment never comes.
- **`Due`** gives a document that a timed transition would move
  `since`, the moment of that timer. An `at` is one filter like a
  guard, and an index on the date it names serves it; an `at` that
  calls a function of a state is evaluated document by document. An
  `after` compares the moment of entry, which has no index, so every
  document of the state is read.
- **What it costs**, on the machine of ADR-0035. A commit of a document
  whose state two timers leave, neither of which has come, costs what
  it cost: `BenchmarkTransitionTimers` reads as `BenchmarkTransition`,
  155 allocations and 8.38 KiB. `Due` over 100,000 documents of which
  100 are due, on disk: 103 µs for an `at` over an indexed date, 6.9 ms
  for an `after`. The list of an eventless guard is as it was, 92 µs.
- **The suite** is `conformance/statechart/timed.json`: a reminder that
  repeats, an escalation from a moment that does not move, a deadline
  that is changed while the document waits, a document that leaves
  before its time, a guard beside a timer, and a blank `at`.
  `conformance/rules/timers.json`, the scripts of item 17 with the
  worker in them, comes with the wake-up.

The wake-up, items 10 to 12 and the suite of item 17, was built on
2026-11-12. It closes the firing half and the step. The owner ruled one
point, the first below; the others were settled.

- **Who a wake-up acts as: the person whose commit queued it** (the
  owner's ruling, 2026-11-12). Item 12 names the commit that began the
  stay, and item 11 has a commit that changes a date queue a row for
  the new moment. The two meet when Ann sends an invoice and Bob later
  moves its due date: the wake-up for the new date is Bob's commit's.
  Ruled: it acts as Bob. `USER()` in the rules of the move is his, what
  they do to other documents is done with his rights, and the event
  names him as actor and his change of the date as its cause. It is the
  rule of ADR-0044, item 11, for every row of the outbox, and nothing
  more is stored. Where no date was changed, that commit is the one
  that began the stay, as item 12 says.
- **Which wake-ups a command queues.** Those of the stay it leaves the
  document in, and not of a state its chain only passed through. For a
  stay the command began, one for each timed transition leaving the
  state whose moment is still to come; a moment that has come was asked
  in the command's own chain, and a blank `at` has none. For a stay the
  command found, one for each `at` whose moment is another than it was
  before the command; an `after` counts from the entry and is not
  queued again.
- **A class that gains a timer** queues nothing when it is defined. A
  document gets its wake-ups when a commit next adopts it into the new
  version, as who made that commit, and `Migrate` adopts them all. One
  whose time came before that is listed by `Due`.
- **The row.** Its action is `timer`, a name no rule can use. Its
  `rule` is the path of the transition, `/transitions/2`; its
  arguments are the state, the target and the name of the transition;
  its `due_at` is the moment. It is listed by `Outbox` and in
  `<Class>_actions` with the document's other rows.
- **Time breaks a chain of actions.** A wake-up has depth 0, and what
  its move queues has depth 1 (ADR-0044, item 6). A reminder repeats
  for years without reaching the bound, which is there to stop actions
  that set each other off at once.
- **The touch is no commit of its own.** It writes what fires in the
  chain and nothing else: no version and no event for the touch. The
  first transition that fires carries the row's key and names the event
  that queued the wake-up as its cause; a further one in the same chain
  names the first, as in any commit. When nothing fires, nothing is
  written.
- **A wake-up that is done appends no `action_run`.** When a transition
  fired, its event is the record, and the row names it under
  `outcome_event`. When nothing was to do, nothing happened to the
  document. A wake-up that dies gets `action_failed` like any action.
- **Which stay a wake-up belongs to.** It does nothing when the document
  is in another state, or entered its state after the row was queued.
  When both happened in one second, it is the row's stay unless the
  document has moved since the version the row was queued with.
- **A wake-up stands outside the order of its document's actions**
  (ADR-0044, item 3). One that waits for a moment a month away holds
  back no action of the document, and an action that waits for another
  attempt holds back no wake-up: the move is the document's own, and no
  receiver waits for it in order.
- **The business date of a timer's move** is the day it fires, in UTC
  by the database's clock. Nobody is there to give another, and the day
  of the commit that queued the wake-up may be a month past.
- **A document an older version of the class computed** is adopted by
  the transition that fires, in the one version it writes. When nothing
  fires it is left as it is.
- **A move that is refused** leaves the wake-up dead at once, as
  ADR-0044, item 4, has it for a refusal: a rule of the transition
  whose action fails, or a chain past its bound. The document has not
  moved and stays due. A commit that touches it fires the same
  transition in its chain and is refused the same way, as with an
  eventless transition whose rule fails, until what refused the move is
  mended.
- **A guard beside a timer that does not hold when the time comes**
  leaves nothing waiting. A commit of the document asks it again, and
  when the guard comes to hold with no commit, `Due` lists the document.
- **The worker is told of a wake-up only when it comes before its next
  look.** A commit that queues an action wakes the worker, since an
  action is due at once. One that queues only wake-ups leaves it asleep
  unless the moment is earlier than the second its sleep ends at: it
  finds the row then. So a commit of a class with timers costs the
  worker nothing.
- **What it costs**, on the machine of ADR-0035, on disk. A transition
  into a state that two timers leave queues two rows: 95 µs and 205
  allocations, where the same transition without timers takes 60 µs and
  155 (`BenchmarkTransitionTimers`), about 18 µs and 25 allocations for
  each wake-up. A timer end to end, the wake-up that is due, the touch,
  the transition it fires and the wake-up of the stay that begins: 170
  µs, some 5,800 timers a second on one writer (`BenchmarkTimer`, which
  joins the gate). A wake-up that finds its document in another state:
  107 µs, of which the look at the queue is most (`BenchmarkTimerIdle`).
  The gated benchmarks keep their allocations: the look at what is due
  reads no column more, and a wake-up reads what it needs of its own
  row.
- **The suite** is `conformance/rules/timers.json`: the scripts of item
  17, with the clock moved and the outbox drained, and the wake-ups
  each commit queues with their moments and who they act as.

The last step of the phase measured the record on 2026-11-12, on disk
(`docs/perf/v0.4.0.md`).

- **`Due` over 1,000,000 documents**, 100 of them due: 0.11 ms with a
  guard or an `at` over an indexed date, inside the 10 ms of the
  indexed filter; 90 ms for a guard over a date with no index; 76 ms
  for an `after`; 9.9 µs a document for a guard that does not
  translate. With every document due, the call lists its 10,000 in
  1.67 s.
- **Timers fire** at 4,501 a second over 200,000 documents. A wake-up
  that finds nothing to do: 11,774 a second. The commit that queues a
  wake-up: 0.35 ms at p99.
- **The life of a document with a long history.** The read is of every
  event of the document, 0.6 µs a version: 0.13 ms for a refresh of a
  document with 100 versions, 6 to 8 ms with 10,000. A summary of the
  life stored with the document would make the read constant. It is
  not built.
- **The filter pays for who and where from.** An indexed filter takes
  17 µs where v0.3.0 takes 13 µs on the same day: four more columns are
  read with each document, and the ten facts of a life are bound with
  each statement, which a filter over a class never reads.

## Consequences

- **Four eyes, and the like, are one line.** The access rule of
  `approve` says `CREATEDBY() <> USER()`. A purchase goes back to
  `LASTEDITOR("draft")`. An invoice that was `sent` three times says so
  with `ENTRIES("sent")`. Who first approved it is
  `FIRSTENTEREDBY("approved")`, and when it last came out of review is
  `LASTLEFT("review")`.
- **Mining and formulas read one set of facts.** `Invoice_states` has
  every stay with who began it and who ended it, by which transition,
  from which state and how; the functions answer the same for one
  document. How much of a process runs by itself is a count over
  `entered_how`. A transition back into the same state is a stay of its own
  in both, so a reminder shows as a step.
- **A database made before this record does not open**, as with every
  change of the engine schema before 1.0: there is no upgrade path, and
  the database is made again. The new columns could have been filled
  from the history, since every version kept its actor. The owner said
  on 2026-11-11 that no database exists yet, so the standing rule
  stays.
- **`created_by`, `updated_by`, `state_entered_by` and
  `state_entered_from` are reserved names.** A class with a variable of one of those names is refused.
- **A timer is evidence like any move.** The log shows who set the
  timer, by beginning the stay or by changing its date, that the time
  came, and what the move did. It shows the move as that person's, with
  the cause beside it. Whoever reads the log for what a person did at a
  moment has to read the cause too.
- **A reminder restarts its own state's timers.** The transition back
  into `waiting` begins a new stay, so every `after` out of `waiting`
  counts again from it. An escalation that must not be pushed back by
  reminders is written with `at`, from a moment that does not move:
  `ADDDAYS(FIRSTENTERED("waiting"), 14)`.
- **Time costs a row.** A document that enters a state with timed
  transitions queues one row for each. A class without them queues
  nothing.

## Performance

- The functions of the row are free: no allocation, as `STATE()` and
  `ID()`. The benchmarks of the gate keep their allocations, and a class
  that uses no function of items 5 and 6 does what it did.
- The functions of a state cost one read of the document's own events
  per command: under 100 µs for a document with 100 versions, the cold
  budget of ADR-0016. A benchmark in the `store` package proves it.
- A commit that queues one wake-up stays inside the 2 ms of a
  transition commit (ADR-0006, ADR-0012). A wake-up that finds nothing
  to do is one read and one update.
- `Due` over a class of 100,000 documents, of which 100 are due, with a
  guard that compares an indexed variable: within the indexed-filter
  budget of ADR-0012. The last step of the phase measures it, and the
  rate at which timers fire, and `docs/perf/v0.4.0.md` reports both.
