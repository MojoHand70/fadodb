# v0.4.0: rules, actions, formulas that read other documents, and time

Measured on 2026-11-12 on main after #72, with Go 1.26.11, linux/amd64,
on an Intel Core Ultra 9 275HX with 24 threads and 31 GB available to
the Linux VM (WSL2, kernel 6.18), the database files on the virtual disk
that holds the home directory, ext4 on a VHD on NVMe. An fsync of 4 KB
cost 1.0 ms at the median and 1.8 ms at p90 that day, measured with
200 writes and fsyncs of one block from Python; on the day of
[v0.3.0](v0.3.0.md) it cost 0.5 ms and 1.3 ms. The in-memory benchmarks
are the mean of three runs; the campaigns run once and report
percentiles. The budgets are those of
[ADR-0043](../adr/0043-rules-inside-the-commit.md),
[ADR-0044](../adr/0044-the-outbox.md),
[ADR-0045](../adr/0045-actions-are-an-extension-point.md),
[ADR-0046](../adr/0046-formulas-that-read-other-documents.md) and
[ADR-0047](../adr/0047-time.md), with the rows of Phases 2 and 3 run
again on the same build.

Every budget holds. Four things are slower than a reader might expect
and have no budget; they are named under
[What is slow](#what-is-slow).

## Rules inside the commit

| Benchmark | What | Time | Allocations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rules | the rules of a transition out of a state: three fire, a set of three values with the recalculation after it, a condition that is false, an emit | 118 ns | 0 |
| Triggered | two triggered variables at their trigger, one of which an auto formula reads | 60 ns | 0 |
| Transition | one named transition with a guard, write and event, the database on tmpfs | 56.5 µs | 155, 8.4 KB |
| TransitionIdleRules | the same on a class with ten rules, none of which the commit raises | 55.8 µs | 155, 8.4 KB |
| TransitionRuled | a transition that fires three rules and writes a custom event | 61.1 µs | 171, 9.3 KB |

Rules that do not fire cost nothing: they are indexed by their trigger
when the class is compiled, and the two benchmarks above read the same.
Rules that fire cost their compute, 118 ns for the three here, and what
they write: the custom event is one more row. On disk the transition
with three rules, two sets, a custom event and a triggered variable
takes 0.076 ms at the median, where the plain transition of the commit
campaign takes 0.062 ms.

## The outbox and actions

A commit queues an action as a row of the outbox in its own
transaction. The worker, or `Drain`, runs each row in a transaction of
its own: what the action does, the mark of the row and the event of the
outcome.

| Benchmark | What, a transition and the drain of what it queued | In memory | On disk | Allocations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outbox | an action of the engine: a create | 233 µs | 301 µs | 459, 23.0 KB |
| OutboxRegistered | a Go function of the host program that does nothing | 215 µs | 279 µs | 490, 22.0 KB |
| OutboxDeclared | a declared HTTP action, with a sender that answers at once | 209 µs | 291 µs | 549, 25.6 KB |
| DeclaredAction | the same with a real round trip to a receiver in the same process | 0.53 ms | | 633, 35.8 KB |
| OutboxEach10 | ten creates queued with `each` | 1.67 ms | 2.13 ms | 3,323, 167.7 KB |

What a row of the outbox costs before it does anything is the look at
the queue and the mark, a transaction: 103 µs in memory
(`BenchmarkTimerIdle`, below). A declared action adds its templates,
the JSON of the request and of the reply and the event of the outcome,
under 50 µs, no more than calling a Go function, within the noise of
the two benchmarks. The network is the rest, 0.3 ms on the loopback.
Ten creates with `each` cost ten rows and ten runs, about 0.2 ms each
end to end.

## Formulas that read other documents

The pattern of parallel approvals: a purchase counts the approvals that
name it, 100 of them, half granted, through the index of the variable
that names the purchase.

| Benchmark, on disk | 100,000 approvals | 1,000,000 approvals | Allocations |
|---|---|---|---|
| LookupClass, the count | 10.6 µs | 10.6 µs | 29, 1.2 KB |
| LookupClassSum, the sum of their amounts | 12.9 µs | 13.0 µs | 34, 1.3 KB |
| RefreshClassCount, the whole command: the count, the guard, the commit | 87 µs | 96 µs | 160, 8.8 KB |

A lookup through an index does not grow with the class. One that no
index narrows reads every document: 85 ms over 1,000,000 approvals in
the campaign, 85 µs per thousand, where
[ADR-0037](../adr/0037-explicit-tables-and-the-compiled-lookup.md)
measured 35 µs per thousand rows of a table; a document is a wider row.
`"index": true` on the variable the lookup compares is the answer.

Over a table, since #62: a count of ten rows of a key
takes 2.6 µs, an exact sum 3.9 µs, a sum of numbers 10.5 µs
(`BenchmarkLookupCount`, `BenchmarkLookupSumExact`,
`BenchmarkLookupSumNumber`).

## Time

### The life of a document in a formula

The functions of the row, `CREATEDAT()`, `ENTEREDBY()` and the others,
read columns of the document and cost nothing. The functions of a
state, `ENTRIES("review")`, `LASTEDITOR("draft")` and the others, read
the document's own events once for a command.

| Versions of the document | Read of its moves alone | Read of every change, for an editor |
|---|---|---|
| 100, the read alone (`BenchmarkLifeStates`, `BenchmarkLifeEditors`) | 43 µs, 222 allocations | 89 µs, 444 allocations |
| 100, the whole refresh on disk | 0.13 ms | 0.17 ms |
| 1,000, the whole refresh | 0.57 ms | 0.79 ms |
| 10,000, the whole refresh | 6.2 ms | 8.4 ms |

The read grows with the history, about 0.6 µs for each version. A class
that calls no function of a state reads nothing.

### `Due`

`Due` lists the documents of a class that a commit would move now. Of
each class 100 documents are due; the file is on disk
(`BenchmarkDue`).

| `Due` | 100,000 documents | 1,000,000 documents |
|---|---|---|
| a guard on an indexed date | 0.10 ms | 0.11 ms |
| a timer `at` an indexed date | 0.11 ms | 0.11 ms |
| a guard on a date with no index | 9.0 ms | 90 ms |
| a timer `after` a time in the state | 7.7 ms | 76 ms |
| a guard on a function of a state, evaluated document by document over a hundredth of the class | 9.9 ms | 99 ms |
| a guard on an indexed date when every document is due: the first 10,000 | 79 ms | 1.67 s |

With an index the list does not grow with the class. Without one it
reads every document of the state. A guard that calls a function of a
state does not translate to SQL and costs 9.9 µs for each document of
its state.

### Timers

| Benchmark | What | In memory | On disk | Allocations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TransitionTimers | a transition into a state that two timers leave: two wake-ups queued | 84 µs | 102 µs | 205, 11.8 KB |
| Timer | a timer end to end: the wake-up that is due, the touch, the move as a version with its event, the next wake-up | 167 µs | 204 µs | 394, 17.1 KB |
| TimerIdle | a wake-up that finds its document in another state | 103 µs | 134 µs | 172, 5.4 KB |

A wake-up costs the commit that queues it about 14 µs and 25
allocations. A document whose timers have not come pays one addition
for an `after` and one evaluation for an `at` in each of its commits.

## A million documents moved from one state to another

Every document's move is a commit of its own, with its version, its
history row and its event, so the log holds the evidence for each. What
a million moves take follows from the rows above:

| What moves the documents | Rate | A million documents |
|---|---|---|
| a command, `Transition` for each | 11,539 a second | 87 seconds |
| the same with three rules firing on each move | 10,159 a second | 98 seconds |
| a timer whose moment comes for all at once, moved by the engine | 4,501 a second | 3.7 minutes |

Other writers take their turn between two of these commits, and reads
go on. A command that moves many documents in one transaction, as
`CreateMany` creates them at 21,269 a second against 7,896 one by one,
does not exist yet. It is planned after this release, with a record of
its own.

## The filter, run again

`BenchmarkFilter` of v0.3.0, on disk, after a load of one commit per
document:

| Filter | 100,000 documents | 1,000,000 documents |
|---|---|---|
| `number = "T-…"`, indexed, one document | 17.1 µs | 16.6 µs |
| the same with the read right on `number` | 17.8 µs, +3.8% | 17.6 µs, +6.0% |
| `number >= "T-…" AND number < "T-…"`, indexed range, ten documents | 41.0 µs | 40.9 µs |
| the same with the read right on `number` | 44.4 µs, +8.4% | 41.9 µs, +2.6% |
| `priority = 3 AND owner = "u7"`, plain columns, first ten | 0.57 ms | 0.52 ms |
| `Count` of `priority = 3`, a plain column, the whole table | 6.7 ms | 72.5 ms |

The read right stays within its 15%. The filter itself is slower than
in v0.3.0: the tag v0.3.0, built and run on the same day, gives 13.3 µs
and 180 allocations for the indexed equality over 100,000 documents,
where this build gives 17.1 µs and 205. Two things of ADR-0047 are in
it. Every document read now carries who created it, who changed it, who
moved it into its state and where it came from, four more columns to
read for each row. And every statement binds the ten facts of a
document's life, which a filter over a class never reads: 11 of the 25
allocations. The budget of the indexed filter is 10 ms.

## What is slow

None of these breaks a budget of the records. Each is a cost that
grows with something, and is here so that nobody finds it by surprise.

1. **A formula that reads the life of a document with a long
   history.** 6 to 8 ms a command at 10,000 versions, since the read is
   of every event of the document. Documents of a hundred versions pay
   0.1 ms. A summary of the life stored with the document would make
   the read constant; it is not built.
2. **`Due` with a backlog.** When 1,000,000 documents are due at once,
   each call takes 1.67 s to list its 10,000: SQLite sorts the ids of
   all that match before it returns the first.
3. **`Due` for an `after`.** The moment a document entered its state
   has no index, so the list reads every document of the state, 76 ms
   over 1,000,000. An index would cost every transition of such a class
   one more index write. The engine's own wake-ups do not use `Due` and
   do not pay this.
4. **The filter, against v0.3.0.** 2 to 4 µs more for an indexed
   filter, as the section above says.

## The three evaluators

The conformance suite holds 1,450 evaluation cases in 16 suites, 141
accepted and 89 rejected syntax cases, and 237 accepted and 222
rejected binding cases in 11 suites; every evaluation case passes
exactly on the Go VM, on SQLite through modernc.org/sqlite and on
DuckDB through the `analytics/` module. The statechart suites under
`conformance/statechart/` grew from five scripts and 85 steps to eight
and 171, with `stays`, `due` and `timed`. New in Phase 4 are the suites
under `conformance/rules/`, 21 scripts and 477 steps that the store
runs with their principals, their clock, their actions and their
drains: the specification of ADR-0043 to ADR-0047 for the server and
the browser VM to run with their phases.

## What the records measured on the way

- [ADR-0044](../adr/0044-the-outbox.md): the first implementation, #54,
  measured a create through the outbox at 205 µs in memory, a transition
  and its drain; it is 233 µs today.
- [ADR-0045](../adr/0045-actions-are-an-extension-point.md): #58
  measured a declared action at 15 µs more than a Go function, and the
  real round trip in the same process at 0.5 ms.
- [ADR-0046](../adr/0046-formulas-that-read-other-documents.md): #63
  measured the count over a class at 10.7 µs, the sum at 12.8 µs and
  the whole refresh at 80 µs, over 100,000 approvals on disk; the
  aggregates over a table at 2.5 µs for a count, 3.7 µs for an exact
  sum and 10 µs for a sum of numbers.
- [ADR-0047](../adr/0047-time.md): the read of a document's life, 42 µs
  for the moves and 85 µs with the editors, at 100 versions; `Due` at
  90 µs with an index, where it was 12 ms before the guard was compiled
  into the filter and the filter learned to use the index of a date
  compared with the clock; a wake-up at about 18 µs and 25 allocations
  in the commit that queues it, and a timer end to end at 170 µs. The
  finding of #71, on the way to the wake-up: the worker read the clock
  twice in each look, and a row whose second began between the two
  readings waited a minute.
