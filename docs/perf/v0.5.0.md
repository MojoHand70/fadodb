# v0.5.0: who may see and do what, and for whom

Measured on 2026-11-13 on the branch of the phase's ninth step, with Go
1.26.11, linux/amd64 and SQLite 1.62.0, on an Intel Core Ultra 9 275HX
with 24 threads and 31 GB available to the Linux VM (WSL2, kernel 6.18),
the database files on the virtual disk that holds the home directory,
ext4 on a VHD on NVMe. An fsync of 4 KB cost 1.0 ms at the median and
1.2 ms at p90 that day, measured with 200 writes and fsyncs of one block
from Python; on the day of [v0.4.1](v0.4.1.md) it cost 1.1 ms and
1.5 ms. The in-memory benchmarks are the mean of three runs; the
campaigns ran once and report percentiles. The budgets are those of
[ADR-0049](../adr/0049-who-may-see-create-change-and-delete-a-document.md),
[ADR-0050](../adr/0050-person-agent-or-service-and-for-whom.md),
[ADR-0051](../adr/0051-who-may-define-operate-and-query.md),
[ADR-0052](../adr/0052-system-groups-and-the-content-administrator.md)
and [ADR-0053](../adr/0053-acting-for-somebody.md), with the rows of
Phases 2, 3 and 4 run again on the same build.

Every budget holds. Asking who may costs a commit about a hundredth of
a millisecond at p99 and no allocation, and a caller who acts for
somebody pays one lookup in memory. Two things are slower than a reader
might expect and have no budget; they are named under
[What is slow](#what-is-slow).

## Who may, inside a commit

The same transition, a named one with a guard, by four callers. The
database is in memory here, so the write is cheap and the differences
between the rows are of the compute.

| Benchmark | Who moves the document | Time | Allocations |
|---|---|---|---|
| TransitionOwned | the owner, on a class with an owner and no rules | 51.7 µs | 163, 8.9 KB |
| TransitionAccess | the owner, on the same class with a read rule and an update rule | 51.2 µs | 163, 8.9 KB |
| TransitionFor | somebody who acts for the owner, checked against a substitution | 50.8 µs | 165, 9.2 KB |
| TransitionOverride | a content administrator, past both rules, with a reason | 51.2 µs | 170, 9.3 KB |

A commit asks the read rule, then the update rule, then the read rule
again for the document it answers with: three evaluations, about 30 ns
each, and no allocation. The times of the four rows differ by less
than one run differs from the next; the allocations are exact.

- **The two allocations of a "for"** are the id acted for, written on
  the event since ADR-0050. With the check switched off, for a host
  that keeps its own substitutions, the count is the same 165: the
  check allocates nothing.
- **The seven of an override** are what it keeps: the places of the
  rules lifted and the reason, on the event. An ordinary commit writes
  two empty values there and allocates nothing for them.

The parts of the check, measured alone:

| Benchmark | What | Time | Allocations |
|---|---|---|---|
| GuardLoop | one guard evaluated, the cost of one rule | 29.7 ns | 0 |
| WriteCheck | the write rights of a set, variable by variable | 243 ns | 0 |
| Project | a document projected for a caller, variable by variable | 1.70 µs | 0 |
| Rules | the rules of a transition out of a state, three fire | 102 ns | 0 |

The file was 7.2 GB at the end, and 1,000,000 events kept an override
with its reason. The maxima of the rows that write are the truncating
checkpoint of the log (ADR-0035), as in the earlier campaigns.

- **The rules cost a commit 0.009 ms at p99**, 0.164 ms against
  0.155 ms, and nothing a caller would notice at the median.
- **A caller who may not see a document is answered as fast as one who
  may.** The rule is evaluated either way, and the answer gives nothing
  away by its time.
- **A substitution given is a commit of two versions**: the document is
  created planned and moves to active in the same command, which is why
  it takes 0.084 ms where a transition takes 0.057 ms.
- **The engine read a pair from the database 2,002 times** in the whole
  campaign, of four million calls with a "for": once per commit that
  wrote a substitution while the assistant kept working. That read
  goes through the index on `by` and takes 0.028 ms at the median with
  a million substitutions in the class.
- **A refused "for" costs 0.4 µs.** The engine remembers that a pair
  has no substitution as it remembers one that has.

## What is slow

Neither of these breaks a budget of the records. Each is a cost that
grows with something, and is here so that nobody finds it by surprise.

1. **A caller's own documents, sorted, when they own many.** The
   first ten of a caller's 10,000 tickets take 10.7 ms: the index on
   the owner finds the 10,000, and the filter sorts them by id before
   it returns ten. With 1,000 tickets it took 0.35 ms in the record.
   It costs the same with the condition written out, so the read rule
   adds nothing here; an index on the owner and the id together would
   make it constant, and a class cannot ask for one yet.
2. **Another writer while batches run**, as in [v0.4.1](v0.4.1.md):
   single sets made while batches of 1,000 moves ran back to back
   waited 69 ms at p90 and 76 ms at p99, since the writer's lock is
   not handed over in the order of arrival. The slowest waited 609 ms,
   the size of the checkpoint that every row's maximum shows, where
   v0.4.1 saw 120 ms. It is unchanged, and not decided.

One finding of [ADR-0049](../adr/0049-who-may-see-create-change-and-delete-a-document.md)
stands as it was: the store keeps no statistics, so SQLite chooses
between two indexes without knowing which finds fewer rows. The filter
works around it for the read rule. Keeping statistics is a decision
about every database and is not taken.

## What the benchmark gate found on the way

The gate allows no allocation more and 5% of time (ADR-0030). The gate,
and the same comparison run by hand before a push, found three things
in this phase, two costs and one placement:

- **A map of a class looked up at every evaluation.** A class kept the
  variables of each formula in a map by formula, and a lookup in a Go
  map is slower once the map has more than eight entries. Four rules
  more took the benchmark's class past eight, and a guard went from
  35.6 ns to 39.2 ns. A formula now carries the variables of the class
  it was bound for: a guard takes 29.7 ns, where the tag v0.4.1 gives
  34.5 ns, and the rules of a commit 102 ns where it gives 116 ns.
- **The path of a rule built before the rule was asked.** The place of
  a rule in the class, as a refusal names it, was put together on
  every check: ten allocations on a write check that had none. It is
  built only after a rule has refused.
- **One benchmark that the gate read slower, and this machine did
  not.** `BenchmarkRecalculate` is the compute of a commit, which the
  ninth step does not touch. The gate's runner read it 5.8% slower on
  the branch, over the limit, and 3.1% slower on a second run, inside
  it. On this machine the gate read 150.8 ns and 151.4 ns for the two
  trees, no significant difference. The benchmark has two speeds,
  151 ns and 157 ns, by where the linker puts its code, in every tree,
  and the two builds place it differently. It is not the class the
  engine now brings: the same binary reads the same with one more
  class in its database. What is left is the placement, and that is a
  reading of the evidence and not a proof.

## How to run it

```
FADODB_BENCH_DIR=/path/on/disk FADODB_BENCH_COMMITS=1000000 go test -count=1 -timeout 2h -v -run 'TestAccessCampaign|TestCommitCampaign|TestRulesCampaign' ./internal/store/
FADODB_BENCH_DIR=/path/on/disk FADODB_BENCH_DOCS=1000000 go test -run '^$' -bench '^BenchmarkFilter$' -benchmem ./internal/store/
go test -run '^$' -bench '^Benchmark(Transition|TransitionOwned|TransitionAccess|TransitionFor|TransitionOverride|GuardLoop|Project|WriteCheck|Rules|Transitions10|Outbox|Timer)$' -benchmem -count=3 ./internal/store/
```

The three campaigns took 9, 15 and 23 minutes, and the filter
benchmark 5. Point `FADODB_BENCH_DIR` at the disk the numbers are meant
for, never at `/tmp`, as [the index of this directory](README.md) says.
